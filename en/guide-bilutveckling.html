Car development: getting the most out of every krona

Every team has two cars with five components each. Upgrading them the right way is the difference between a team that climbs and a team that stands still despite big spending. Here we go through the maths behind the upgrades and the most common traps.

The five components

Car 1 and Car 2 are upgraded completely separately, and each component has an index from 0 to 100. The components contribute differently in qualifying and the race, and the effect scales with the character of the track:

ComponentQualifyingRaceTrack type where it shines
EngineModerateBiggest contributionLong tracks
AerodynamicsBiggest contributionModerateTwisty tracks (mainly in qualifying)
Tyres–Large contributionTwisty tracks
ChassisLarge, evenLarge, evenAll tracks
GearboxSmall, evenModerate, evenAll tracks

Two things stand out. First, the chassis is the most reliable component: it contributes solidly in both qualifying and the race regardless of the track. Second, the tyres only show up in the race – they don’t affect the qualifying lap at all, but they decide how well the car holds its pace through the race.

The cost grows with the square

Each upgrade raises the component by 5 index steps and costs 20,000 kr + index² × 30, based on the index before the upgrade. That gives a steeply rising curve:

From indexCost of the next +5
020,000 kr
2032,000 kr
4068,000 kr
60128,000 kr
80212,000 kr
95290,750 kr

Adding up the steps makes the difference even clearer. Taking a component from 0 to 50 costs about 414,000 kr in total. Taking the same component through the last 25 steps, from 75 to 100, costs almost 1.2 million kr – nearly three times as much for half the improvement.

The conclusion is simple: build wide before you build high. Five components at 60 normally give more performance per krona than three at 90 and two at 20.

Order of priority for a new team

  1. Lift every component off the bottom. Below index 30 upgrades are so cheap that they almost always pay off.
  2. Chassis and engine next – the chassis because it helps everywhere, the engine because it gives the most in the race, where the points are handed out.
  3. Aerodynamics and tyres according to the tracks in your group. If you race on many twisty tracks, they are worth more.
  4. Gearbox last – it contributes evenly but the least.

Remember there are two cars. A team with one top car and one weak car often loses to a team with two even cars, because the standings count the sum of both cars’ points.

Timing: upgrades take a day

The money is charged immediately, but an upgrade takes a day before the new level kicks in, and each component can only have one upgrade running at a time. Two practical consequences:

  • Start the upgrades no later than Tuesday evening if you want them to count in Wednesday’s qualifying.
  • You can upgrade all five components on both cars in parallel. Ten upgrades a day are therefore possible – if the budget allows.

Plan around the regulations

After every season new regulations come into force that halve one component for all teams. The order is fixed and rotating: before season 2 the Tyres are halved, before season 3 the Engine, before season 4 the Aerodynamics, before season 5 the Gearbox, before season 6 the Chassis – and then it starts over.

This is one of the most underrated parts of the game. Imagine you upgrade the engine from 80 to 85 late in season 2 for 212,000 kr. At the season change the engine is halved – and of your five extra index steps only 2.5 remain. You have paid full price for half the effect, and you’ve done it on the most expensive part of the cost curve.

Rules of thumb:

  • Avoid expensive upgrades of the component that is next in line to be halved during the last weeks of the season.
  • Spend the money on the other four components during that period.
  • After the halving the component is cheap to rebuild – that is when you should invest in it.

Since the halving hits every team equally, it isn’t a punishment against you – it’s an opportunity for whoever plans better than the competition.

Damage – and why an Aggressive driving style costs money

After every race 0–3 cars are randomly damaged. Damage permanently lowers a component by 10–70 percent of its value, and there is no repair – the component is rebuilt with ordinary upgrades.

An example: an engine at 80 that is damaged by 30 percent ends up at 56. Building it back to just over 80 costs about 760,000 kr. Drivers with an Aggressive driving style run a considerably higher risk of damaging the car than drivers with a Balanced or Defensive style – that risk should be weighed in when you choose drivers, especially if the car is already expensive to maintain.

A small consolation: since upgrades are cheaper the lower the index, rebuilding is always cheaper than the original build-up of the same steps.

Summary

  • Build wide: lift the weakest components first.
  • Chassis and engine give the most in almost every situation.
  • Keep both cars even.
  • Start upgrades no later than the day before qualifying.
  • Save on the component that will be halved at the next season change.
  • Expect damage – and think about your drivers’ driving style.
